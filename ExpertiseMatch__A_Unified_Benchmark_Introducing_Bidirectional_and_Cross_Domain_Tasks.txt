## Abstract

[cite_start]Expertise matching plays a pivotal role in academic peer review and industrial innovation. [cite: 4] [cite_start]However, existing research suffers from three critical limitations: (1) a predominant focus on matching papers to reviewers, overlooking the equally important reverse direction matching reviewers to papers which is essential for reviewer engagement; [cite: 5] (2) [cite_start]confinement to academic domains, which hinders cross-domain generalization; [cite: 6] [cite_start]and (3) a fragmented evaluation landscape that precludes fair and systematic model comparison. [cite: 7] [cite_start]To address these gaps, we present EXPERMATCH, the first unified benchmark for expertise matching that supports bidirectional matching (e.g., papers reviewers) as well as across domains (e.g., academia vs. industry). [cite: 8] [cite_start]Built on rigorously curated datasets with graded relevance labels, EXPERMATCH enables comprehensive evaluation across diverse expertise matching paradigms. [cite: 9] [cite_start]Our extensive experiments reveal a fundamental trade-off between matching effectiveness and computational efficiency: while dense embedding models achieve the highest accuracy, they incur prohibitive inference costs. [cite: 10] [cite_start]In contrast, a context-aware multi-factor model delivers competitive performance at substantially lower latency. [cite: 11] [cite_start]These results demonstrate that the optimal model choice is highly dependent on task-specific requirements. [cite: 12] [cite_start]Notably, EXPERMATCH 's insights have been deployed on a large-scale innovation platform at a leading tech company, demonstrating real-world impact. [cite: 13]

[cite_start]**Keywords:** Peer Review Reviewer Assignment Expertise Matching Benchmark Dense Retrieval. [cite: 14]

### 1 Introduction

[cite_start]Expertise matching the task of identifying individuals with specific skills for a given need is a cornerstone of the modern knowledge-based economy. [cite: 16] [cite_start]In academia, the integrity of peer review hinges on accurately matching papers to qualified reviewers [17], while in industry, the ability to rapidly assemble expert teams is a critical driver of innovation [7]. [cite: 17] [cite_start]Despite its universal importance, research in this area has been fragmented and limited in scope, often addressing a narrow slice of the problem without a holistic perspective. [cite: 18] [cite_start]The most significant limitation has been the framing of academic expertise matching as a unidirectional problem: given a paper, find suitable reviewers. [cite: 19] [cite_start]Early systems, such as the Toronto Paper Matching System (TPMS) [4], and their successors focused exclusively on this "Paper-as-Query" paradigm. [cite: 51] [cite_start]While crucial for conference organizers, this narrow view neglects the inherent bidirectionality of the academic ecosystem. [cite: 52] [cite_start]Reviewers are not passive resources but active participants who need to discover papers relevant to their interests, particularly during the critical bidding phase. [cite: 53] [cite_start]Persistent challenges, including low reviewer engagement and superficial reviews 15], underscore the urgent need for systems that also cater to the reviewer's perspective. [cite: 54] [cite_start]The reverse "Reviewer-as-Query" direction has long been an underexplored research gap, primarily due to a lack of suitable evaluation data. [cite: 55] [cite_start]However, the recent emergence of a gold-standard dataset [18] with explicit, graded expertise scores now makes it possible to rigorously benchmark this task for the first time. [cite: 56] [cite_start]Furthermore, the core challenge of matching textual needs to expert profiles is not unique to academia. [cite: 57] [cite_start]Industrial settings face a parallel "Project-as-Query" problem, yet this cross-domain application has rarely been studied in conjunction with its academic counterpart, hindering knowledge and model transfer. [cite: 58]

[cite_start]To address these limitations and foster a more holistic approach, we introduce EXPERMATCH, a comprehensive and unified benchmark. [cite: 59] [cite_start]EXPERMATCH is built upon three pillars that systematically expand the scope of the problem: (1) the conventional P-to-R task, (2) the novel R-to-P task, and (3) the cross-domain J-to-R task. [cite: 60] [cite_start]To ensure a clear scientific focus, our benchmark is strictly scoped to the fundamental challenge of similarity computation, separating it from the subsequent combinatorial problem of assignment optimization. [cite: 61]

This work makes the following primary contributions:
1. [cite_start]We introduce EXPERMATCH, the first unified benchmark to formalize and evaluate expertise matching across bidirectional and cross-domain tasks. [cite: 63]
2. [cite_start]We establish the first comprehensive baseline results for two novel tasks: the R-to-P task, leveraging a gold-standard dataset with explicit expertise scores, and the J-to-R task, which bridges the gap between academic and industrial applications. [cite: 65]
3. [cite_start]We conduct an extensive evaluation of diverse models from classic IR methods to state-of-the-art embedding models and provide a deep analysis of the critical trade-offs between model quality and efficiency. [cite: 66]
4. [cite_start]To ensure full reproducibility, we will release our complete evaluation codebase and the processed data files for all public datasets used in our experiments. [cite: 67]

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. [cite_start]Section 2 reviews prior work. [cite: 68] Section 3 details our benchmark framework. [cite_start]Section 4 describes the experimental setup. [cite: 69] [cite_start]Section 5 presents and analyzes the results. [cite: 69] [cite_start]Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper. [cite: 69]

### 2 Related Work

[cite_start]Prior work in expertise matching has evolved from simple lexical methods to complex, multi-factor frameworks. [cite: 71] [cite_start]This review focuses on content-based models to highlight the fragmented evaluation landscape that motivates our work. [cite: 72] [cite_start]While powerful, structure-based approaches using GNNs [9] are outside the scope of this benchmark. [cite: 73]

#### 2.1 Evolution of Content-Based Models

* [cite_start]**Lexical Models.** Early systems relied on bag-of-words representations such as TF-IDF vectors [4]. [cite: 75] [cite_start]Although efficient, these methods are hindered by the well-known semantic gap, as they fail to capture synonymy and polysemy. [cite: 76]
* [cite_start]**Context-free Embeddings.** To bridge this semantic gap, subsequent work employed context-free word embeddings like Word2Vec [10]. [cite: 77] [cite_start]By representing words as dense vectors, these models capture semantic similarity that extends beyond mere lexical overlap. [cite: 78]
* [cite_start]**Topic Models.** Concurrently, an alternative line of research utilized topic models, such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) [3], to represent documents in a shared, low-dimensional thematic space. [cite: 79] [cite_start]This approach enables matching based on conceptual alignment rather than direct textual similarity. [cite: 80]
* [cite_start]**Contextualized Language Models.** The advent of Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) like BERT [6] marked a paradigm shift. [cite: 81] [cite_start]This catalyzed the development of specialized models such as SciBERT [2] for the scientific domain and, more recently, state-of-the-art variants that incorporate citation information. [cite: 82] [cite_start]Models like SPECTER2 [16] and SciNCL [12] learn document-level embeddings engineered to capture nuanced inter-document relevance. [cite: 83]

#### 2.2 Multi-Factor Unified Models

[cite_start]The current frontier transcends single-signal approaches by unifying multiple factors into cohesive frameworks. [cite: 87] [cite_start]The Chain-of-Factors (CoF) model [21] is a paradigmatic example, positing that relying on any single factor is insufficient. [cite: 88] [cite_start]CoF jointly considers semantic, topic, and citation signals through a novel instruction-guided architecture and a coarse-to-fine inference process. [cite: 89] [cite_start]It represents the state-of-the-art in holistic matching and serves as a critical baseline in our benchmark. [cite: 90]

#### 2.3 The Need for a Unified Benchmark

[cite_start]The progression towards sophisticated, multi-factor models like CoF [21] has significantly advanced matching accuracy. [cite: 92] [cite_start]However, this has also fragmented the evaluation landscape, with different models tested on bespoke datasets using varying metrics, making fair comparisons difficult. [cite: 93] [cite_start]Critical questions remain: under what conditions do advanced semantic models truly outperform simpler topic models, and what is the tangible benefit of multi-factor frameworks over strong single-signal baselines? [cite: 94] [cite_start]A unified benchmark is essential to answer these questions. [cite: 95] [cite_start]We introduce EXPERMATCH to address this gap, enabling rigorous, multifaceted, and cross-domain evaluation of the models discussed herein. [cite: 95]

### 3 The EXPERMATCH Benchmark Framework

[cite_start]This section formally defines the core tasks, datasets, and evaluation metrics of the EXPERMATCH benchmark. [cite: 97]

#### 3.1 Generalized Task Definition

[cite_start]We formulate expertise matching as a ranking problem. [cite: 99] [cite_start]Given a query q (e.g., a paper) and a set of candidates $C=\{c_{1},...,c_{n}\}$ (e.g., reviewers), the objective is to define a scoring function $f(q,c)$ that ranks candidates by their relevance to the query. [cite: 99] [cite_start]Ground truth is provided as triplets (q. [cite: 100] [cite_start]c, y), where y is the match label. [cite: 100] [cite_start]Crucially, all models are evaluated in a zero-shot inference setting. [cite: 101] [cite_start]without any task-specific training on these triplets. [cite: 101]

#### 3.2 Core Tasks

[cite_start]This generalized definition is instantiated into three core tasks, which are summarized in Table 1. [cite: 103]

[cite_start]**Table 1. The three core tasks in the EXPERMATCH benchmark.** [cite: 109]

| Task | Description | Query ( ) | Candidate (c) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **P-to-R** | The classic academic task of finding suitable reviewers for a given paper. | A paper $(p_{i})$ represented by its title and abstract. | A reviewer $(r_{j})$ represented by their prior publications. |
| **R-to-P** | The reverse task of recommending relevant papers to a reviewer. | A reviewer $(r_{j})$ represented by their prior publications. | A paper $(p_{i})$ represented by its title and abstract. |
| **Jto--R** | The cross-domain task of matching researchers to industrial projects. | A project (jk) represented by its description, goals, and keywords. | A researcher (st) represented by their profile and publications. |

[cite_start]The benchmark comprises six datasets spanning academic and industrial domains, as summarized in Table 2. [cite: 105]

* [cite_start]**Stelmakh et al.** The cornerstone of our R-to-P task is the dataset from Stelmakh et al. [cite: 112] [cite_start][18], which contains self-assessed reviewer expertise scores on a 1-5 scale for papers in their broad research area that they read to a reasonable extent in the last year. [cite: 113] [cite_start]This high-quality, explicit feedback is crucial for reliably evaluating the R-to-P task. [cite: 114] [cite_start]We refer to the original 1-5 scores as the raw version and create two binarized versions: soft (scores 4.25, 4.5, 4.75, 5.0 are positive) and hard (scores € 4.75,5.0 are positive). [cite: 115]
* [cite_start]**NIPS.** The NIPS dataset [11] for the P-to-R task is built upon manual annotations. [cite: 116] [cite_start]It contains 34 papers from NIPS 2006 and 190 reviewers, with relevance scores on a 0-3 scale provided by community researchers. [cite: 117] [cite_start]We use the original 0-3 scores as the raw version and binarize them into soft (scores {2,3} are positive) and hard (score = 3 is positive) versions, following the protocol from Zhang et al. [cite: 118] [cite_start]21]. [cite: 119]
* [cite_start]**KDD and SciRepEval.** The KDD dataset, introduced by Zhang et al. [cite: 120] [cite_start][21], and the SciRepEval dataset [16] are larger-scale benchmarks for the P-to-R task, also based on manual annotations with a 0-3 rating scale. [cite: 121] [cite_start]As the original graded scores for these datasets are not publicly available, we use the binarized 'soft' and 'hard' versions released by Zhang et al. [cite: 125] [cite_start][21]. [cite: 126] [cite_start]From these, we reconstruct a three-level raw version: non-matches are mapped to 0, labels present only in the 'soft' version are mapped to 1, and labels present in the 'hard' version are mapped to 2. [cite: 126]
* [cite_start]**SIGIR.** The SIGIR dataset [8] contains 73 papers from SIGIR 2007. [cite: 127] [cite_start]Its relevance labels are derived from aspect-level similarity. [cite: 127] [cite_start]Each paper and reviewer is assigned one or more of 25 aspects, and the relevance is the Jaccard similarity of their aspect sets. [cite: 128] [cite_start]We replicate the procedure from Zhang et al. [cite: 129] [cite_start][21] by treating the discretized Jaccard similarity score as the raw label and subsequently deriving soft and hard versions. [cite: 129]
* [cite_start]**WIZ1000.** This is our in-house industrial dataset for the J-to-R task. [cite: 130] [cite_start]The queries are project descriptions from a corporate research fund, and candidates are researchers. [cite: 131] [cite_start]A positive label indicates that a researcher applied for the corresponding project. [cite: 132] [cite_start]To simulate a realistic, large-scale retrieval scenario, we constructed the candidate set in two stages. [cite: 133] [cite_start]First, for each of the 609 project queries, we initially sampled 1000 negative candidates (researchers with $>10$ publications in $CS/AI)$ from the OpenAlex [13] database. [cite: 134] [cite_start]Second, all unique candidates sampled in the first stage were aggregated to form a single, shared negative pool. [cite: 135] [cite_start]For the final evaluation, the candidate set for a given query consists of its set of positive candidates (one or more researchers who applied for the project) and all candidates from this global negative pool. [cite: 136] [cite_start]This dataset only has a binary soft label. [cite: 137]

[cite_start]In our zero-shot evaluation, we use the standard test sets for all public datasets; [cite: 138] [cite_start]the entire WIZ1000 dataset is used for testing. [cite: 139] [cite_start]To ensure reproducibility, we will release our evaluation code and the specific data files for all public datasets. [cite: 139] [cite_start]The WIZ1000 dataset cannot be released due to privacy constraints. [cite: 140]

#### 3.4 Evaluation Metrics

[cite_start]We employ two sets of ranking metrics to comprehensively evaluate model performance across different label types. [cite: 143]

* [cite_start]**Graded/Multi-level Labels ('raw versions).** For these labels, we use metrics sensitive to the rank of correctly ordered items. [cite: 144] [cite_start]Our primary metric is Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@k), defined as: [cite: 145]
    $NDCG@k = \frac{DCG@k}{IDCG@k}$ where $DCG@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{rel_i}{\log_2(i+1)}$
    [cite_start]Here, $rel_{i}$ is the relevance score of the item at rank i, and IDCG@k is the DCG score of the ideal ranking. [cite: 151] [cite_start]For the R-to-P task, which uses the fine-grained Stelmakh dataset, its insightful rank correlation scores motivated us to also report Kendall's Tau and Spearman's Rho. [cite: 152]
* [cite_start]**Binary Labels ('soft and 'hard versions).** For these labels, we use standard information retrieval metrics, with Mean Average Precision (MAP) as the primary metric, which provides a single-figure measure of quality across recall levels. [cite: 155] [cite_start]For a set of queries Q, MAP is the mean of the Average Precision (AP) scores: [cite: 156]
    $MAP = \frac{\sum AP(q)}{|Q|}$ where $AP = \frac{\sum P(i) \times rel(i)}{\text{number of relevant documents}}$
    [cite_start]In the AP formula, $P(i)$ is the precision at rank i, and $rel(i)$ is an indicator function that is 1 if the item at rank i is relevant and 0 otherwise. [cite: 162] [cite_start]For all metrics, scores are reported at $k=10$. [cite: 163]

### 4 Experimental Setup

This section details the experimental setup for our benchmark. [cite_start]We first introduce the suite of baseline models, which range from classic information retrieval methods to state-of-the-art embedding models. [cite: 165] [cite_start]We then describe our evaluation of different representation and matching strategies for these embedding-based approaches. [cite: 166]

#### 4.1 Baseline Models

[cite_start]We selected a comprehensive suite of models to establish a robust comparative analysis, categorizing them into three main groups. [cite: 168]

* [cite_start]**Classic IR Baselines.** These models represent traditional, non-neural approaches. [cite: 169] [cite_start]We include two strong baselines: BM25 [14], a standard bag-of-words retrieval function based on TF-IDF, and the seminal TPMS [4]. [cite: 169] [cite_start]TPMS utilizes a vector space model where a reviewer's profile is an aggregated document of their publications, and the matching score is the dot product between the paper's and reviewer's TF-IDF vectors. [cite: 170]
* [cite_start]**Embedding-based Models.** This category encompasses the dominant paradigm of encoding texts into dense embeddings for similarity computation. [cite: 171] [cite_start]We group them by their pre-training corpora. [cite: 172] [cite_start]For **Scientific-Domain Embeddings**, we evaluate SciBERT [2], pre-trained on scientific papers; [cite: 172] [cite_start]SPECTER [5], fine-tuned on the citation graph; [cite: 173] [cite_start]SciNCL [12], a strong contrastive learning baseline; [cite: 173] [cite_start]and SPECTER2 [16], a powerful successor to SPECTER. [cite: 174] [cite_start]For SPECTER2, we test its Base model and its Adhoc, Proximity, and Classification adapterbased variants. [cite: 174] [cite_start]For **General-Purpose Embeddings**, we use COCO-DR [19], an advanced zero-shot dense retriever, and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [20], a state-of-the-art multilingual model. [cite: 175]
* [cite_start]**Advanced Baselines.** This group includes more complex models from recent literature. [cite: 178] [cite_start]We replicate a lightweight GRU-based 1] model, which is the only baseline requiring task-specific training. [cite: 179] [cite_start]We also replicate CoF [21], a state-of-the-art method that uniquely combines semantic, topic, and citation factors for matching. [cite: 180]

#### 4.2 Representation and Matching Strategies

[cite_start]A crucial challenge in expertise matching is representing a candidate who is defined by a set of N documents (their publications). [cite: 182] [cite_start]Given a query embedding q and a candidate's set of document embeddings $\{c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{N}\}$, we systematically evaluate two families of strategies for computing the final match score. [cite: 183]

* [cite_start]**Embedding-first Strategies.** These strategies first aggregate the candidate's document embeddings into a single representative vector, $c_{agg}$, before computing similarity. [cite: 184] [cite_start]We test aggregation via mean pooling (Emb-Avg, where $c_{agg}= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i})$ , element-wise max pooling (Emb-Max), and a weighted average (Emb-W.Avg). [cite: 185] [cite_start]For Emb-W.Avg, the embeddings of the top-k documents, ranked by cosine similarity to the query, are aggregated. [cite: 186] [cite_start]The weights for this average decay linearly from 1.0 (for the top-ranked document) down to 0.5 (for the k-th ranked), and are then normalized to ensure they sum to one. [cite: 187] [cite_start]The final score is computed as $cos(q,c_{agg})$. [cite: 188]
* [cite_start]**Score-first Strategies.** These strategies first compute individual cosine similarity scores between the query and each of the candidate's documents and then aggregate these scores. [cite: 189] [cite_start]We evaluate aggregation via the mean of scores (Score-Avg, score $=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}cos(q,c_{i}))$ the maximum score (Score-Max), and a weighted average of the scores (Score-W.Avg). [cite: 190] [cite_start]This method applies the same normalized, linear-decay weighting scheme described above directly to the top-k ranked cosine similarity scores to compute the final score. [cite: 191]

### 5 Results and Analysis

[cite_start]This section presents a series of experiments designed to comprehensively evaluate the baseline models on the EXPERMATCH benchmark. [cite: 193] [cite_start]Our analysis first identifies the optimal matching strategy for each embedding-based model via a robust meta-analysis, then uses these individually optimized configurations to conduct a fair comparison across all models and tasks. [cite: 194] [cite_start]The efficiency implications of these choices are analyzed in the final part of this section. [cite: 195] [cite_start]The analysis is guided by the following research questions: [cite: 196]

* [cite_start]**RQ1:** Which models achieve the best performance on the core matching tasks? [cite: 197]
* [cite_start]**RQ2:** How effectively do models handle the classic Paper-to-Reviewer (P-to-R) task? [cite: 198]
* [cite_start]**RQ3:** How effectively do models handle the novel Reviewer-to-Paper (R-to-P) task? [cite: 206]
* [cite_start]**RQ4:** How well do models generalize to the cross-domain Project-to-Researcher (J-to-R) task? [cite: 208]
* [cite_start]**RQ5:** What are the efficiency trade-offs of these models? [cite: 209]

#### 5.1 RQ1: Overall Performance on Core Tasks

[cite_start]To answer our first and most central question, we present the main performance results of all models across the three core tasks in Table 3. [cite: 211] [cite_start]This table serves as the primary leaderboard for our benchmark, providing a high-level overview of model capabilities. [cite: 211] [cite_start]A primary finding of our analysis is that no single model consistently outperforms others across all tasks. [cite: 212] [cite_start]The performance hierarchy shifts depending on the task, indicating that the optimal choice is highly context-dependent. [cite: 213] [cite_start]For the novel R-to-P task, which relies on high-quality graded scores, the specialized embedding model SPECTER2 (Proximity) is the top-performing model. [cite: 214] [cite_start]In the classic P-to-R task, after normalizing scores across the four datasets to ensure fairness, SPECTER2 (Base) achieves the best overall performance. [cite: 215] [cite_start]The advanced multi-factor model, CoF, achieves the second-highest performance, outperforming most embedding models. [cite: 216] [cite_start]In the J-to-R task, the general-purpose models Qwen3-Emb and SciNCL tie for the top spot, highlighting their strong generalization capabilities. [cite: 217] [cite_start]This varied performance landscape underscores the value of our multifaceted benchmark in revealing the specific strengths and weaknesses of different approaches. [cite: 218]

#### 5.2 RQ2: In-depth Analysis of the P-to-R Task

[cite_start]While the average scores in Table 3 suggest a competitive landscape, a deeper dive into the individual P-to-R datasets, presented in Table 4, reveals a complex and nuanced performance hierarchy. [cite: 225] [cite_start]A key finding is that model performance is highly dataset-dependent, and no single model consistently outperforms others across all settings. [cite: 226] [cite_start]The advanced CoF model, for instance, exhibits a specialized performance profile, excelling in specific contexts. [cite: 227] [cite_start]It establishes itself as a top performer on the SIGIR and SciRepEval datasets, securing the top rank in several settings across both. [cite: 228] [cite_start]However, this specialization is context-dependent; on the NIPS dataset, it is consistently outperformed by various embedding models like SciNCL and SPECTER2. [cite: 229] [cite_start]Its performance on KDD is also less remarkable. [cite: 230] [cite_start]The SIGIR dataset presents a notable anomaly. [cite: 231] [cite_start]The performance of nearly all models, especially the embedding-based ones, plummets in the 'raw' (NDCG@10) setting. [cite: 231] [cite_start]This suggests that SIGIR's labels, which are derived from abstract aspect-level similarity rather than direct textual relevance, pose a unique challenge that current semantic models struggle to address effectively. [cite: 232] [cite_start]This highlights a critical insight from our benchmark: the notion of a single "best" model is misleading, and performance is highly contingent on the specific characteristics and label semantics of the dataset. [cite: 233]

#### 5.3 RQ3: In-depth Analysis of the R-to-P Task

[cite_start]For the novel R-to-P task, we leverage the unique, fine-grained labels of the Stelmakh dataset, with detailed results presented in Table 5. [cite: 235] [cite_start]The results demonstrate the superiority of embedding-based models in this setting, which tests pure semantic understanding. [cite: 235] [cite_start]SPECTER2 (Proximity) outperforms all other models, achieving the highest scores on nearly all metrics, including NDCG@10, the crucial rank correlation coefficients, and soft MAP. [cite: 236] [cite_start]This finding suggests that its pre-training objective, which is specifically designed to identify related or "proximal" documents, is well-aligned with the requirements of the "reviewer-as-query" paradigm where the goal is to find relevant papers for a given expert. [cite: 239] [cite_start]The robust performance of SPECTER2 (Base) as the top model in the strict 'hard' setting, and the strong showing of SciNCL and CoF as runnersup on other metrics, further reinforces this trend. [cite: 240] [cite_start]The models that excel are those with powerful, general-purpose scientific document representations. [cite: 241] [cite_start]This contrasts sharply with the P-to-R task, confirming that the two tasks are not symmetric and require different modeling strengths, which validates the necessity of our bidirectional benchmark. [cite: 242]

#### 5.4 RQ4: Generalization to the Cross-Domain J-to-R Task

[cite_start]To evaluate how well models generalize from the academic to the industrial domain, we analyze their rank stability, visualized in Figure 2. [cite: 247] [cite_start]A significant rank shift is immediately apparent, revealing that strong performance in the academic P-to-R task does not guarantee success in the industrial J-to-R task. [cite: 247] [cite_start]A key finding is the high robustness of certain general-purpose models. [cite: 248] [cite_start]Qwen3-Emb and SciNCL, despite not being the top two performers in the academic domain, rise to the top in the industrial setting, demonstrating superior generalization capabilities. [cite: 249] [cite_start]In contrast, the top academic performers show mixed results. [cite: 250] [cite_start]SPECTER2 (Base) (rank 1 in P-to-R) remains a strong performer in the J-to-R task, while the advanced model CoF (rank 2 in P-to-R) experiences a notable drop in rank. [cite: 250] [cite_start]This suggests that its specialized, multi-factor architecture may be overfitted to the structure of academic papers and fails to adapt well to the different vocabulary and format of industrial project descriptions. [cite: 251] [cite_start]This highlights a key principle: for cross-domain applications, a robust and general semantic foundation may be more critical than a complex architecture tailored to a specific source domain. [cite: 252]

#### 5.5 Analysis of Matching Strategy Trade-offs

[cite_start]To identify the optimal matching strategy, we analyze six approaches (detailed in Section 4.2) by their performance and efficiency. [cite: 292] [cite_start]Rather than averaging potentially skewed scores, we use a robust Ordinal Rank Aggregation method. [cite: 293] [cite_start]For this analysis, we applied all six strategies to every embedding-based model. [cite: 294] [cite_start]We then ranked the strategies for each model based on their resulting performance (NDCG) across all P-to-R datasets for different rank cut-offs (ke {5, 10, 25, 40, 55}). [cite: 295] [cite_start]The final performance score for each strategy is its average rank across all these scenarios. [cite: 296] [cite_start]Efficiency is measured by average query latency. [cite: 297] [cite_start]The results, summarized in Table 6, reveal a clear quality-efficiency trade-off. [cite: 297]

* [cite_start]**Performance.** The analysis reveals that weighted average strategies are the most robust. [cite: 301] [cite_start]Score-W.Avg achieves the top rank, closely followed by Emb-W.Avg. [cite: 302] [cite_start]This indicates that weighting information by its query similarity is a superior method for amplifying relevant signals. [cite: 303] [cite_start]We also observe a clear two-tier performance gap: the top four strategies significantly outperform Emb-Avg and Emb-Max, which suggests that simple mean or max pooling of embedding vectors is a lossy representation method that discards crucial expertise signals. [cite: 304]
* [cite_start]**Efficiency.** As expected, the simplest strategies, Emb-Avg and Emb-Max, are the most efficient, proving 2x to 4x faster than alternatives. [cite: 305] [cite_start]Conversely, the high-performing Emb-W.Avg is the least efficient due to its multi-step computational process: calculating initial similarities for weights, performing a weighted sum, and then computing a final similarity score. [cite: 306]
* [cite_start]**The Quality-Efficiency Trade-off.** The juxtaposition of these ranks in Table 6 provides clear guidance for practitioners. [cite: 307] [cite_start]For quality-first applications where accuracy is paramount, Score-W.Avg is the top-performing strategy, justifying its moderate efficiency cost. [cite: 308] [cite_start]For efficiency-first systems requiring real-time responses, Emb-Avg is the only viable choice, despite its lower performance. [cite: 309] [cite_start]For most standard applications, however, Score-Max offers a balanced trade-off, representing a Pareto-optimal solution. [cite: 310] [cite_start]It achieves a high performance rank (4th) with a high efficiency rank (3rd), presenting an effective compromise between quality and computational cost. [cite: 311]

#### 5.6 RQ5: Overall Quality-Efficiency Trade-offs

[cite_start]Having analyzed the micro-level trade-offs between matching strategies, we now broaden our scope to address RQ5 from a macro perspective, comparing all evaluated models in our benchmark. [cite: 313] [cite_start]We plot each model's representative P-to-R performance against its computational efficiency, providing a global view of the quality-efficiency landscape. [cite: 314] [cite_start]Performance is measured by the average NDCG@10 across the four academic P-to-R datasets (from Table 3), while efficiency is the average time to process a single query, as detailed in Table 7. [cite: 315] [cite_start]The results, visualized in Figure 3, reveal three distinct clusters of models, each representing a different trade-off profile. [cite: 316]

* [cite_start]**High-Efficiency, Baseline-Performance:** Occupying the bottom-left corner, the classic IR models BM25 and TPMS are, by an order of magnitude, the fastest methods. [cite: 317] [cite_start]Their near-instantaneous query times set a clear efficiency benchmark. [cite: 318] [cite_start]However, this speed comes at the cost of significantly lower matching quality compared to neural approaches, establishing them as strong but limited baselines. [cite: 319]
* [cite_start]**High-Performance, High-Cost:** The top-right quadrant is densely populated by the Embedding models. [cite: 320] [cite_start]This cluster demonstrates a substantial performance improvement over classic IR models, forming a clear frontier of matching quality. [cite: 321] [cite_start]Within this group, models like SPECTER2 (Base) represent the state-of-the-art in pure quality. [cite: 322] [cite_start]This superior performance, however, requires a substantial increase in computational resources, with query times being 20-30 times slower than classic IR. [cite: 323]
* [cite_start]**The Balanced Middle-Ground:** The CoF model occupies a distinct position between the two primary clusters. [cite: 326] [cite_start]It is significantly faster than the entire family of embedding models yet delivers a performance score that is highly competitive, achieving the second-highest rank overall. [cite: 327] [cite_start]This positions CoF as an exceptional balanced choice for systems that require high-quality matching but cannot afford the latency of state-of-the-art embedding models. [cite: 328] [cite_start]In contrast, the GRU-based model is suboptimal, as it is both slower and less effective than CoF. [cite: 329]

[cite_start]In conclusion, our analysis provides clear guidance for practitioners: for maximum speed, classic IR models are unmatched. [cite: 330] [cite_start]For state-of-the-art quality, scientific-domain embedding models like SPECTER2 (Base) are the premier choice. [cite: 331] [cite_start]For systems that require a balance between high performance and reasonable efficiency, CoF offers a practical solution. [cite: 332]

### 6 Conclusion

[cite_start]In this work, we presented EXPERMATCH, the first unified benchmark for formalizing and evaluating bidirectional (P-to-R and R-to-P) and cross-domain (J-to-R) expertise matching. [cite: 336] [cite_start]Our evaluation revealed that the optimal model is highly context-dependent, with no single model achieving the best performance across all tasks. [cite: 337] [cite_start]For the R-to-P task, the domain-specific SPECTER2 (Proximity) was the top-performing model. [cite: 338] [cite_start]For the conventional P-to-R task, SPECTER2 (Base) achieved the highest overall performance, while in the cross-domain J-to-R task, the general-purpose models Qwen3-Emb and SciNCL showed the strongest generalization, tying for the top rank. [cite: 339] [cite_start]Furthermore, our analysis identified a critical quality-efficiency trade-off. [cite: 379] [cite_start]While state-of-the-art embedding models define the performance frontier, they incur substantial computational costs. [cite: 379] [cite_start]In contrast, the CoF model offers a practical balance, delivering performance comparable to top embedding models at a fraction of the latency. [cite: 380] [cite_start]This benchmark provides a clear framework for practitioners to select models based on specific requirements for quality and efficiency. [cite: 381] [cite_start]To foster future research, we will release our complete evaluation codebase and all processed public datasets. [cite: 382] [cite_start]This research has been deployed on a large-scale innovation platform at a major technology corporation, where it supports industry-academia collaboration. [cite: 383]
